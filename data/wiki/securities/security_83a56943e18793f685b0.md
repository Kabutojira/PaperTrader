## Business economics

Texas Instruments designs and manufactures analog and embedded-processing semiconductors. Its economics depend on broad product exposure, long product lives, internally owned manufacturing, factory utilization, and disciplined capital investment. Internal manufacturing can provide supply control and unit-cost benefits at high utilization, but it also creates fixed-cost and capital-intensity risk when semiconductor demand weakens.

In its [Q2 2026 financial release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/ti-reports-second-quarter-2026-financial-results-and-shareholder-returns-302832506.html) (`source_web_8fadba48a2d1bce2032c6e23`), the company reported:

- Revenue of USD 5.463 billion, up 23% year over year and 13% sequentially.
- Operating profit of USD 2.310 billion, up 48% year over year.
- Net income of USD 1.980 billion and diluted EPS of USD 2.14.
- Analog revenue of USD 4.365 billion, up 26%, and Embedded Processing revenue of USD 788 million, up 16%.
- Trailing-12-month operating cash flow of USD 8.667 billion.
- Company-defined trailing-12-month free cash flow of USD 6.534 billion, which includes CHIPS Act incentive proceeds under the company's stated non-GAAP definition.
- Q3 guidance of USD 5.65-6.15 billion in revenue and USD 2.23-2.57 in EPS.

Management characterized growth as broad and led by industrial, data center, and automotive markets. These are verified issuer statements and results; their continuation is not verified.

## Financial position and contrary evidence

At June 30, 2026, the issuer reported USD 3.660 billion of cash, USD 3.341 billion of short-term investments, USD 4.605 billion of inventory, USD 1.149 billion of current debt, and USD 12.903 billion of long-term debt. Inventory remains substantial even after improving from the prior-year comparison, and cash plus short-term investments do not exceed total reported debt.

Material contrary considerations are:

1. Broad Q2 growth may include cyclical recovery or customer restocking that does not persist.
2. Internally owned factories can magnify margin pressure when utilization falls.
3. Inventory and product-obsolescence risks remain relevant in a demand reversal.
4. Capital expenditures and other strategic uses of cash can prevent reported operating cash flow from translating fully into per-share value.
5. Competition, trade policy, customer inventory adjustments, and manufacturing execution can reduce normalized earnings.

## Thesis

The thesis is that broad analog exposure, improved semiconductor demand, and higher factory utilization sustain normalized earnings and cash generation above trough levels. Q2's revenue, segment growth, operating leverage, and cash flow support that possibility. The counter-thesis is that the market already capitalizes a durable recovery before demand, utilization, and returns on manufacturing investment have been demonstrated across a full cycle.

The supplied market snapshot records a USD 276.48 close on 2026-10-16. That price is above the base fair value below. It is contextual market data, not a substitute for fair value and not evidence that the base scenario must rise.

## Valuation method and scenarios

A normalized-EPS multiple is used because the company is profitable but exposed to semiconductor cycles and utilization-driven margin changes. Reported Q2 EPS and Q3 guidance provide an earnings anchor, while future normalized EPS and valuation multiples are explicit assumptions.

| Bear | 25% | USD 7.50 | 20x | USD 150 | Demand reversal, customer inventory correction, weak factory absorption, lower margins, and greater financial risk. |

Per-share arithmetic is straightforward: USD 7.50 × 20 = USD 150; USD 9.50 × 27 = USD 256.50; and USD 11.00 × 34 = USD 374. The 25%/50%/25% probabilities place the greatest weight on continued but normalizing recovery while preserving balanced cyclical tails. These bounded scenarios are not price forecasts.

## Downside and invalidation

The bear scenario captures both earnings contraction and multiple compression. Invalidate the recovery thesis if broad end-market growth reverses for multiple reporting periods while inventory rises; profitability deteriorates despite higher revenue; normalized EPS cannot sustain at least USD 7.50; cash generation weakens materially relative to investment needs; debt increases without corresponding per-share earnings growth; or management cuts guidance because demand or customer inventory corrections are materially worse than assumed.

The base valuation would require upward revision if verified results demonstrate sustainable EPS near or above USD 11.00, improving cash conversion, and no material increase in financial risk. A single strong quarter would not be sufficient by itself.
