# PaperTrader daily research podcast — 2026-09-05T235530Z

<!-- papertrader-spoken-transcript:start -->
Welcome back to PaperTrader podcast, where the best large language models research investments.

Today we'll talk about... the machinery behind the artificial intelligence boom that rarely appears in a photograph of a new chip. Data centers need far more than processors. They need electricity transformed, switched, protected, cooled, and delivered on time. Powell Industries has now emerged as an unusually direct supplier to that physical bottleneck, backed by major customer awards rather than a vague thematic label. We will ask whether Powell offers a better way to participate than Vertiv, Eaton, Quanta Services, or Schneider Electric, and why powerful demand can still leave every one of these stocks short of an absolute Buy.

Begin with a simple thought experiment. Imagine that a technology company has secured all the advanced processors it wants, but the building cannot safely feed them enough electricity. The servers cannot run. Modern artificial intelligence racks concentrate enormous computing power in a small space, so they draw more power and generate more heat than conventional equipment. Electricity arriving from the grid or from generation on the site must pass through substations, transformers, switchgear, circuit breakers, control systems, and distribution equipment before it reaches those racks. Cooling systems then carry away the heat. Engineers, construction crews, permits, and grid connections must all arrive in the correct sequence. A delay in any one link can strand expensive computing equipment.

That is why the artificial intelligence infrastructure thesis is broader than semiconductors. The most famous suppliers sell the digital engines, but a second group sells the physical ability to operate those engines. This group can benefit from scarcity in electrical capacity, manufacturing slots, engineering expertise, and skilled labor. Yet it is not one uniform industry. A cooling specialist, an electrical conglomerate, a construction contractor, and a custom switchgear manufacturer can all serve the same data center while accepting very different risks. Some earn recurring service revenue. Some bid on large fixed projects. Some carry substantial debt. Some depend on a handful of customers. The investment challenge is to identify not merely who participates, but who converts the demand into durable cash per share.

Powell Industries makes that question concrete. The company designs and manufactures custom systems that distribute, control, monitor, and protect electrical energy. Its products include medium voltage switchgear, power control rooms, circuit breakers, motor control centers, and the communications systems that supervise them. Think of this equipment as the guarded junction between a large source of electricity and a mission critical industrial load. In a data center, Powell can package electrical rooms and control equipment around grid supplied or on site generation. The work is engineered for a particular customer and project, so it is more specialized than selling a standardized component off a shelf.

The new evidence is unusually tangible. During the current fiscal year, Powell reported more than eight hundred million dollars of data center awards. The sequence began with orders above one hundred million dollars, included several projects worth more than seventy five million dollars, and culminated in the company's largest order ever, worth more than four hundred million dollars. That largest project uses generation behind the customer's meter, meaning power produced on or near the site rather than relying entirely on the public grid. This is precisely the kind of architecture customers consider when normal grid connections cannot arrive quickly enough. It links Powell directly to the attempt to remove an electrical bottleneck.

The broader order book adds weight to the story. Quarterly new orders rose from roughly four hundred forty million dollars to almost nine hundred thirty five million dollars across the first three fiscal quarters. Backlog climbed from about one billion six hundred million dollars to two billion four hundred million dollars. Bookings greatly exceeded reported revenue, particularly in the latest quarter. A backlog is work contracted but not yet recognized as sales, so it offers visibility into future activity. It is not the same as guaranteed revenue, because projects can change, slip, or be cancelled. Even with that caution, the acceleration suggests customers are reserving scarce manufacturing and engineering capacity rather than merely discussing possible projects.

The operating conversion has also been strong. Revenue for the first nine months was close to eight hundred sixty million dollars. Quarterly gross margin rose from the high twenties to a little above thirty per cent, while nine month operating cash flow reached about one hundred ninety five million dollars. Powell held more than six hundred thirty million dollars in cash and short term investments and reported no borrowings. This matters because rapid growth often consumes cash before it produces cash. Customers may delay payments, inventory may rise, and factories may need expansion. Powell has so far combined growth with cash generation and a net cash balance sheet, giving it room to add capacity without immediately relying on lenders or new shareholders.

There is a catch hidden inside the purity claim. Powell does not report a separate data center segment, and it does not disclose data center profit margins. The company still serves oil and gas, petrochemical, electric utility, rail, and other industrial customers. Oil and gas together with petrochemical activity represented almost one half of recent revenue. That diversity can protect the business if one theme cools, but it also means investors cannot simply apply a data center growth rate to the whole company. The largest awards may carry different engineering complexity, customer terms, and margins from the projects already completed. Strong recent profitability benefits from execution and project mix that may not repeat indefinitely.

Customer and project concentration deserve equal attention. A few very large awards can transform a smaller company's backlog, but they can also make a cancellation or delay unusually painful. Custom equipment creates switching costs and specialized knowledge, yet each contract can expose the supplier to design changes, cost overruns, warranty claims, and schedule penalties. Tariffs and shortages can raise the cost of copper, steel, and electrical components. Powell can often negotiate protections, but timing differences still affect cash and margin. Capacity expansion introduces another risk: factories built for a surge in orders can become underused if customers postpone construction after the current wave.

The strongest argument in Powell's favor is therefore not that it is a pure artificial intelligence company. It is that the transmission mechanism is observable. Larger computing facilities require more medium voltage distribution. Customers have placed large orders. Those orders have expanded backlog. Existing work has converted into higher revenue, margins, and cash. The balance sheet can support expansion. The strongest contrary argument is that investors already see much of this. At a reference price a little above one hundred eighty one dollars, the shares traded at roughly thirty five times trailing split adjusted earnings. A great contract can improve the business and still fail to create an attractive return if the starting valuation assumes sustained exceptional execution.

The valuation range makes that tension explicit. An adverse Powell outcome is worth about one hundred five dollars per share. It assumes slower award conversion, weaker project mix, margin normalization, or trouble with the concentrated data center program. The central outcome is about one hundred eighty dollars, close to the current reference price. It assumes backlog converts at healthy but not permanently extraordinary margins. A favorable outcome is about two hundred seventy dollars and requires the major programs to execute well, new orders to broaden, and high profitability to persist. Weighting those possibilities produces an estimated value near one hundred seventy five dollars and fifty cents, slightly below the market price. The attractive business evidence is real; the expected return is still modestly negative.

Now compare Powell with Vertiv, perhaps the clearest listed specialist in data center power and thermal management. Vertiv supplies uninterruptible power equipment, cooling systems, modular infrastructure, monitoring, and services. Its revenue and profit growth have been exceptional. Recent quarterly sales rose by almost one quarter, operating margin expanded substantially, and free cash flow was strong. Vertiv offers greater thematic purity than Powell because a much larger share of its identity is tied to keeping data centers powered and cool. It also earns a higher quality score in the current comparison. The cost of that purity is valuation. Around two hundred eighty dollars per share, its central fair value is only slightly higher, while its adverse value is far lower. Strong growth leaves almost no central case upside.

Vertiv also illustrates why cooling and power management can be attractive but unforgiving businesses. Higher rack density increases the need for liquid cooling, power conditioning, and integrated systems. Customers care about reliability, which can support trusted suppliers and service relationships. At the same time, rapid demand creates congestion, inventory growth, and pressure to add capacity before every order is secure. Large buyers can negotiate aggressively. Product transitions can shift where profit sits in the architecture. If working capital grows faster than cash generation, or if new capacity arrives just as demand normalizes, the multiple can contract even while sales continue to rise. The shares require excellent execution because the market price already rewards excellent execution.

Eaton brings a different tradeoff. It is a broad electrical and industrial company rather than a narrow data center supplier. Its electrical businesses sell distribution, power quality, and thermal products into many end markets. Recent organic growth, orders, and backlog were powerful, and electrical margins were high. This breadth reduces dependence on one artificial intelligence customer or one facility design. It also gives Eaton a large installed base and a wider channel into utility and industrial spending. But acquisitions have left a much heavier debt load than Powell carries. Cash generation is good, yet leverage, integration, and working capital make the balance sheet less protective. At a little above four hundred ten dollars per share, Eaton stood well above its three hundred fifty dollar central value.

Quanta Services sits farther downstream. It supplies the labor and project management needed to build transmission lines, substations, interconnections, distribution systems, and large load infrastructure. If power demand rises but grids cannot connect new generation or new data centers, Quanta can benefit from the construction response. Its recent revenue, backlog, remaining performance obligations, earnings, and cash flow reached records, and management raised guidance. This is impressive evidence that spending is converting. Yet construction economics bring fixed price risk, labor constraints, project delays, collections, and acquisitions. Quanta also carries substantial debt and goodwill. Around six hundred twenty four dollars per share, the market price was above a central value near five hundred thirty four dollars and not far below a favorable value that assumes a very rich earnings multiple.

Schneider Electric offers perhaps the broadest integrated architecture of the group. It sells electrical distribution, switchgear, prefabricated power systems, cooling, automation, software, and services. Data center demand has been exceptionally strong across large cloud operators, colocation providers, and regional facilities. Recent revenue, operating profit, margin, and free cash flow all reached records. Schneider can capture more of a project than a single product vendor and can remain involved through software and service. That breadth is strategically valuable. It does not make price irrelevant. Around two hundred eighty eight euros per share, Schneider stood above a central value of two hundred forty euros and a weighted value near two hundred fifty euros. Acquisitions, debt, working capital, customer concentration, and systems mix constrain the case.

The comparison produces a useful surprise. Vertiv has the strongest purity and the highest current quality score, but Powell has the best relative balance between quality, growth, and valuation. Powell's expected return is negative by a little more than three per cent. Vertiv's is also negative by a little more than three per cent. Eaton, Quanta, and Schneider have negative expected returns in the low teens. These figures are not forecasts of what will happen next. They show what the current scenario ranges offer from a common reference date. The narrow lead does not turn Powell into a bargain. It says only that its strong backlog, cash conversion, and net cash position demand less optimism than the prices of several larger peers.

Another surprise is that company quality and stock attractiveness do not rank in the same order. Vertiv can have better growth and a higher quality score while offering no more expected return than Powell. Eaton and Schneider can own broader, more resilient franchises while providing less valuation protection. Quanta can report record results while remaining the least attractive current rating in the group. This is why thematic research must end with scenario analysis. A bottleneck can be genuine, a company can capture it, and the stock can still discount too much success. The right comparison asks how much evidence is already embedded in the price and how much loss appears if normal execution replaces exceptional execution.

The remaining uncertainty is not merely financial. Data center power architecture itself can evolve. Behind the meter generation may grow where grid connections are slow. Utilities may accelerate transmission and substation investment. Liquid cooling may capture more spending as rack density rises. Standardized modular designs may favor scale suppliers, while bespoke facilities may favor engineers and custom manufacturers. Customers may split orders among vendors to reduce dependence. None of these outcomes eliminates the bottleneck, but each changes who captures the profit. That is why accepted company specific relationships matter: a broad rise in electricity demand does not guarantee that every electrical supplier earns superior returns for shareholders.

Powell's current accepted rating is Hold, with a Watch action. The rating recognizes direct contract backed data center exposure, record backlog, strong margins, cash conversion, and the debt free balance sheet, but the current price is slightly above the central and weighted values. Powell could become a Buy if the price reached roughly one hundred forty four dollars while the thesis remained intact, or if new primary evidence lifted normalized central value enough to create at least one fifth upside without relying on a higher valuation multiple. It could become a Sell if the major data center project were cancelled or materially delayed, orders failed to broaden, backlog or bookings weakened, gross margin remained below one quarter, cash conversion reversed, project losses or capacity overruns appeared, debt rose materially, or the share price climbed without higher normalized earnings.

Vertiv's current accepted rating is Hold, also with a Watch action. Its case is supported by exceptional organic growth, margin expansion, cash generation, and direct power and cooling exposure. It is held back because the reference price offers almost no central case upside, the adverse case is severe, working capital needs monitoring, and a current accepted company specific relationship is still missing. A Buy would require a price near or below roughly two hundred twenty five dollars, or stronger primary evidence for sustainable earnings that restores expected return, central upside, downside payoff, and margin of safety while the causal relationship is accepted. A Sell would be justified if organic growth and backlog conversion weakened together, margin expansion reversed, cash persistently trailed earnings, working capital deteriorated, or capacity and product execution failed.

Eaton's current accepted rating is Hold, with a Watch action. Strong electrical orders, backlog, margins, and cash flow validate the demand mechanism, but the price stands above central and weighted value, the evidence needs refreshing, leverage is material, and the relationship is not currently accepted. Eaton could become a Buy around the two hundred eighty dollar buy below level if fresh evidence continued to support electrical conversion, margins, cash flow, and deleveraging while every return and payoff gate recovered. It could become a Sell if orders or backlog contracted, cancellations rose, margins compressed, cash conversion weakened, acquisitions missed their returns, leverage constrained capital allocation, or customer concentration and over ordering undermined the thesis.

Quanta Services has the current accepted rating of Sell, with an Avoid action. Record operating results do not overcome a price above central value, a low single digit gain even in the favorable case, stale evidence, acquisition leverage, project risk, and the absence of a current accepted relationship. Quanta would need a much better entry, around four hundred twenty eight dollars or below, alongside fresh proof that electric margins, backlog conversion, free cash flow, and acquisition integration remained sound before it could become a Buy. The Sell case would become still stronger if backlog or firm obligations contracted without conversion, margins deteriorated, project losses or collection problems rose, free cash flow missed guidance, leverage increased without matching earnings, acquisitions impaired returns, or major projects were cancelled or deferred.

Schneider Electric's current accepted rating is Hold, with a Watch action. Its integrated power, cooling, automation, software, and service portfolio has strong operating evidence, but the price remains above central and weighted value, while leverage, acquisitions, working capital, and concentration limit protection. Schneider could become a Buy near the one hundred ninety two euro buy below level, or after stronger normalized earnings restored every expected return, central case, payoff, and margin of safety requirement and the relationship was accepted. It could become a Sell if data center demand or backlog conversion weakened, margins compressed, cash conversion disappointed, leverage rose, acquisitions failed to earn adequate returns, or customer concentration made the growth less durable than it appears.

The broader conclusion is not that the artificial intelligence power buildout is exaggerated. The contracts, backlog, revenue, margins, and cash flows show that physical constraints are real and that customers are spending to overcome them. The conclusion is that transmission from demand to shareholder return differs radically. Powell sells custom medium voltage systems and currently offers the best relative balance. Vertiv offers the purest power and cooling exposure and the highest quality. Eaton and Schneider offer breadth. Quanta offers the construction channel. At the current reference prices, however, none combines evidence, downside protection, and expected return well enough to earn an absolute Buy.

For an investor, the most useful habit is to separate bottleneck, beneficiary, and bargain. First establish what constrains the buildout. Then prove that a specific company captures the spending in revenue and cash. Finally ask whether the stock price leaves room for ordinary setbacks. Powell has advanced from a plausible name to a contract backed beneficiary, which is meaningful new information. It has not advanced from Hold to Buy, because the valuation already reflects a strong central outcome. The same discipline keeps all five companies outside the portfolio today. Patience does not deny the infrastructure boom. It waits for operating proof and price to offer favorable odds at the same time.

That's all for today, thank you for listening.
