# Daily research podcast — August 20, 2026

<!-- papertrader-spoken-transcript:start -->

Welcome back to PaperTrader podcast, where the best large language models research investments.

Today we'll talk about... why growing defense demand can still leave a drone maker short of cash, why a major shareholder selling millions of payment-company shares is not the same as the company diluting its owners, and why a larger cash reserve at a Bitcoin treasury vehicle may come with a hidden bill for common shareholders. These stories share one practical question: when unusual trading volume appears, what has actually changed in the economics of each share?

Start in France with Parrot, a company many people may remember for consumer drones but whose current investment case increasingly depends on professional aircraft. Parrot designs compact drones, cameras, communications systems, software, and image-analysis tools. Its professional products can serve surveying and mapping customers, but the most important new demand comes from governments that want secure, locally controlled reconnaissance equipment. A small drone can give a military unit a view beyond the next building or ridge without risking a crewed aircraft. The strategic attraction is not merely that the aircraft flies. Buyers care about where components come from, whether communications can be trusted, how data are stored, and whether the supplier can deliver during a crisis.

That creates a plausible European sovereign-drone thesis. Governments are rebuilding inventories, shortening vulnerable supply chains, and seeking alternatives to systems whose components or cloud connections may be controlled elsewhere. Parrot has direct evidence that this demand is becoming revenue rather than remaining a policy slogan. In the first half, total revenue reached roughly fifty-seven million euros. Professional microdrone revenue was a little above thirty-seven million euros and grew by nearly one quarter from the previous year. Photogrammetry, the business of turning images into measurements and maps, contributed about twenty million euros and grew more slowly. Gross margin moved above one half of revenue, while recurring operating income turned positive at about two million euros after a loss a year earlier.

Those figures matter because they show operating leverage. When revenue rises faster than recurring costs, an emerging supplier can move from funding a product platform to earning money from it. Parrot also has identifiable catalysts rather than a vague hope for more defense spending. These include conversion of French procurement frameworks into deliveries, progress on a Ukrainian-oriented aircraft program, tender outcomes in South Korea, and selections by other allied customers. If repeat orders arrive, manufacturing and software costs can be spread across more units. A company with control of the aircraft, payload, communications, and software may then retain more of the value than a simple component assembler.

But positive recurring operating income is not the same as positive cash generation. Parrot used about fourteen million euros of operating cash during the half while holding less than nineteen million euros of cash at the period end. Inventory and customer receivables absorbed resources before revenue became collected money. Partner funding added support, including money received after the reporting date, but funding from a shareholder or partner is still a claim on future resources. The share count also rose modestly. That is why the most important test is not whether government demand exists. It is whether contracts turn into repeat deliveries, gross profit, and collected cash before financing needs erode the value belonging to each share.

Liquidity in the stock adds another layer. Only about twenty-one thousand shares changed hands in the latest referenced session, representing roughly two hundred thousand euros of turnover. A quoted price can therefore move sharply without a deep pool of buyers and sellers. The recent upward momentum crossover came after a fall of roughly three tenths from the prior month's level. The shares still sat below both a medium-term and a long-term average. That makes the chart a sign of stabilization after a drawdown, not proof that the market has discovered a new contract or a durable improvement in cash conversion.

The valuation captures both the opportunity and the fragility. The adverse case is about five and a half euros per share, the central case a little above nine euros, and the favorable case about fourteen and a half euros. The market mark was about nine euros and eighty cents. Weighting the cases produces a value close to the central estimate and below the market. The favorable outcome is meaningful, but the adverse outcome is a loss of more than two fifths. A conservative price that allows the required margin of safety is roughly seven euros and twenty-seven cents. At the current mark, investors are being asked to accept procurement concentration, working-capital risk, thin trading, and possible dilution without enough compensation in the central case.

Parrot is therefore currently Hold and Watch. The rating recognizes genuine professional-drone growth and positive recurring operating income, but the weighted value remains below the market and cash consumption, customer concentration, financing dependence, and thin liquidity keep the shares ineligible. Parrot could become a Buy if the price falls to roughly seven euros and twenty-seven cents while major awards convert into repeat deliveries, margins persist, and operating cash flow improves, or if stronger verified cash economics lift the supportable values enough to create the same safety margin. It could become a Sell if professional-drone growth stalls, awards do not become collected revenue, recurring profit reverses without a timing explanation, cash burn forces destructive financing, or dilution overwhelms operating progress.

Now move from aircraft to payment infrastructure. DLocal helps global merchants accept payments and send payouts across emerging markets. Instead of building separate banking connections, licenses, local payment methods, foreign-exchange arrangements, and settlement processes country by country, a merchant can use one platform and contract. That convenience can be valuable because payment systems in these markets are fragmented. The company benefits if more international commerce flows through its network, but its economics depend on keeping a small portion of a very large payment volume while managing regulation, taxes, currencies, settlement liquidity, and concentrated merchant relationships.

The latest operating evidence is strong but not uncomplicated. Quarterly payment volume grew by almost twice the prior-year amount, revenue rose by more than one half, and gross profit increased by nearly one third. Operating profit and net income also advanced, while adjusted free cash flow recovered to nearly seventy million dollars. Management raised guidance for payment volume and gross-profit growth. Yet gross profit earned on each dollar of payment volume fell again, partly because large merchants moved into lower pricing tiers and local payment flows grew. The ten largest merchants had previously represented more than three fifths of revenue. Rapid volume growth is useful only if pricing, operating leverage, and cash conversion preserve enough profit for shareholders.

A burst of unusual trading brought a different issue into view. General Atlantic, a large shareholder, sold nearly ten million Class A shares in the open market at an average price around thirteen dollars and sixty cents. The reporting group still owned more than one fifth of that share class afterward. This was a secondary sale: an existing owner transferred already issued shares to new owners. DLocal did not sell new shares, receive the proceeds, or increase its share count through that transaction. That distinction is essential. Heavy volume caused by a sponsor reducing its stake can affect supply, sentiment, and the future ownership overhang, but it does not mechanically dilute every remaining share.

True dilution works differently. If a company issues new shares, the same business value is divided among more claims unless the cash raised creates enough additional value. A secondary transaction merely changes who owns existing claims. It may still carry information. A knowledgeable sponsor could be diversifying, returning capital to its own investors, or expressing a less optimistic view. But the filing reported no material change to the stated purpose of the investment or to relevant contracts. Without additional evidence, the sale explains why so many shares traded; it does not establish that dLocal's merchants, margins, regulation, or cash flows changed that day.

The market price rose to about fourteen dollars and thirty cents on more than seven million shares. That remained below the existing conservative buy level near fourteen dollars and ninety-six cents. The accepted adverse value is nine dollars, the central value eighteen dollars and seventy cents, and the favorable value twenty-nine dollars and forty cents. Their weighted value is just under nineteen dollars. At the new mark, the central outcome offers roughly three tenths upside and the weighted outcome a little more. The adverse case, however, still loses more than one third. The valuation is attractive enough to deserve attention, but valuation alone does not explain why this company belongs in a coherent investment theme or how its risk should be compared with alternatives.

That missing causal relationship is not bureaucracy. A company can look statistically cheap while the investor lacks a tested reason for why its advantage should persist. For dLocal, the needed work would have to connect its local licenses, integrations, settlement capabilities, and merchant network to a maintained thesis about digital-finance rails, then test whether take-rate compression and concentration weaken that connection. It would also need to distinguish a durable infrastructure advantage from temporary growth driven by a few very large clients. Until that link is accepted, the research can identify favorable economics without turning them into an allocation decision.

DLocal is therefore currently Medium and Watch. Its rating reflects fast payment-volume growth, stronger operating profit and cash conversion, and a market price below the accepted buy-below level, balanced against take-rate compression, merchant concentration, regulatory and currency risks, and the absence of an accepted causal investment relationship. It could become a Buy if that relationship is independently established while the price remains at or below roughly fourteen dollars and ninety-six cents and current evidence on gross-profit growth, cash conversion, guidance, and dilution remains intact. It could become a Sell if persistent pricing pressure overwhelms operating leverage, a major merchant leaves or reprices sharply, cash conversion deteriorates, regulatory or tax action restricts settlement, capital deployment becomes destructive, or the market price moves beyond supportable outcomes without better evidence.

The final story is Strategy, the company formerly known chiefly for enterprise analytics and now dominated economically by a vast Bitcoin treasury. Common shareholders do not simply own a pile of Bitcoin. They own the residual value after debt, preferred securities, financing costs, taxes, and other claims, plus a much smaller software business. The company can issue common shares, preferred shares, or debt; buy or sell Bitcoin; build a dollar reserve; and repurchase securities. These tools may protect liquidity or increase exposure, but every move changes who bears risk and who receives value.

The two newest weekly filings show that machinery in motion. Strategy reported about eight hundred forty thousand Bitcoin and a dollar reserve that rose to roughly four billion eight hundred million dollars. Across the two weeks, it issued a little more than ten million common shares and raised close to one billion dollars. It also repurchased more than two and a half million shares of one preferred series. During the first week it sold about seventeen hundred Bitcoin for a little over one hundred million dollars; during the second it reported no Bitcoin purchase or sale. The reserve grew and one layer of preferred claims declined, but common owners absorbed more shares while Bitcoin holdings edged lower.

Why might management do this? Preferred securities can carry dividends, redemption features, or other obligations that demand cash. Repurchasing them may reduce future claims. A larger dollar reserve can help fund interest, dividends, operations, and commitments without forcing Bitcoin sales during a weak market. Issuing common shares can be beneficial when the market pays more for each new share than the value of the assets and claims attached to it. In that circumstance, the company may raise more value than it gives away. But the arithmetic must be measured per fully diluted common share. A larger reserve or a smaller preferred claim is not automatically accretive if the common-share count grows faster than net assets.

The balance sheet makes that caution unavoidable. At the prior quarter end, debt was several billion dollars and preferred liquidation claims were well into the tens of billions. Bitcoin is volatile and produces no operating cash flow to pay interest or preferred dividends. The software operation generates recurring revenue, but its scale is small beside the treasury. Capital-market access is therefore part of the business model, not a side activity. It works best when investors eagerly buy securities and Bitcoin is stable or rising. It can become dangerous when the asset falls, the premium to underlying value contracts, or new financing becomes expensive at the same time obligations remain fixed.

This also explains why Strategy is not treated like an operating digital-payment rail. Coinbase, a custody platform, or a stablecoin network may earn fees from transactions and services. Strategy primarily provides a leveraged residual claim on Bitcoin shaped by financial claims and issuance policy. Broader adoption of digital assets may help the Bitcoin price, but that does not create payment, custody, or settlement revenue for Strategy. Calling both businesses part of digital finance would hide the very mechanism an investor needs to evaluate.

The market's unusual volume accompanied a gain of a little more than four percent over the measured period, with an especially active final session. The filings offer a plausible reason for attention, but not a clean bullish catalyst. They reveal improved near-term liquidity and reduced preferred claims alongside dilution and a small Bitcoin sale. The accepted adverse value is roughly fifty-four dollars, the central value about one hundred eight dollars, and the favorable value about one hundred seventy-four dollars. The market was near one hundred four dollars. Weighting the outcomes gives a value only fractionally above the quote, and confidence adjustment reduces the already small expected advantage.

Those scenarios depend heavily on Bitcoin assumptions. The adverse case uses a Bitcoin price around forty-five thousand dollars, the central case around seventy thousand, and the favorable case around one hundred thousand, while also assigning a modest value to software. They hold the disclosed Bitcoin quantity and financing claims broadly constant. Further adverse issuance, additional claims, or lower Bitcoin per share could make them optimistic. The central value being close to the market does not mean risk is balanced, because the adverse case loses almost half while the expected gain is negligible. A highly volatile residual claim needs more than a tiny weighted discount.

Strategy is therefore currently Hold and Watch. Its rating reflects deep capital-market access, a larger cash reserve, reduced preferred claims, and substantial Bitcoin exposure, but these positives are offset by current common dilution, treasury turnover, senior claims, downside asymmetry, and almost no expected return at the latest price. It could become a Buy if verified fully diluted Bitcoin and net asset value per common share rise, financing is demonstrably accretive, the reserve covers obligations without harmful issuance, and a refreshed valuation establishes a meaningful margin of safety. The accepted research does not provide a reliable numeric buy boundary because later issuance and fully diluted conversion details remain incomplete, so inventing one would be misleading. It could become a Sell if Bitcoin per fully diluted share keeps falling, financing claims grow faster than assets, the reserve cannot cover obligations without distressed issuance or Bitcoin sales, capital-market access closes during a drawdown, or the market price outruns even supportable favorable outcomes.

Together, these cases show why trading volume is a question, not an answer. At Parrot, the important change is operating progress inside a cash-hungry defense supplier. At dLocal, the volume was largely an ownership transfer that did not alter the issuer's share count. At Strategy, the activity points toward deliberate balance-sheet engineering whose benefits and costs must be measured per common share. The same market statistic can therefore accompany a better business, an unchanged business with a new owner mix, or a reshaped set of financial claims.

The useful investor habit is to trace every event through the economic chain. Ask who paid whom, whether cash entered the company, whether the share count changed, which claims rank ahead of common stock, whether revenue became collected cash, and whether the current price compensates for the adverse case. Parrot still needs contract conversion and cash discipline. DLocal needs a validated causal edge as well as attractive arithmetic. Strategy needs evidence that financial engineering increases value per diluted share rather than merely increasing the size of the treasury. None of the three has produced an actionable position, but each clarifies what evidence would genuinely change the conclusion.

That's all for today, thank you for listening.
