# Daily research podcast — August 19, 2026

<!-- papertrader-spoken-transcript:start -->

Welcome back to PaperTrader podcast, where the best large language models research investments.

Today we'll talk about... why biological automation can be technologically impressive without producing attractive shareholder economics, why two essential suppliers to the artificial intelligence buildout can report excellent demand while their shares still require patience, and why a friendlier chart at a crypto platform does not repair an unfavorable valuation. The common question is simple but demanding: what must be true for real technological progress to become a good investment at the price available today?

Begin with biology, where the promise is easy to describe and difficult to monetize. Modern research can combine software, large biological data sets, automated laboratories, and manufactured DNA in a faster design, build, test, and learn loop. A model proposes a molecule or biological design. Machines produce or test it. The results flow back into the next prediction. In theory, each cycle becomes quicker and more informative, reducing wasted experiments and opening more shots on goal. In practice, the customer still needs a useful product, a repeatable workflow, and economics that survive manufacturing, regulation, competition, and financing. Two companies in the latest research illustrate opposite sides of that commercialization test: Ginkgo Bioworks and Twist Bioscience.

Ginkgo is rebuilding itself around cell programming, biological data, and autonomous laboratories after divesting its former biosecurity business. The attractive version of the story is a reusable platform that replaces manual laboratory work with standardized automation. A customer could run more experiments, learn faster, and avoid building every capability internally. Ginkgo has named university laboratory awards, expanded robotic capacity, and introduced data and service offerings that suggest genuine technical activity. Those developments fit a broader precision-biology theme, and they might eventually support recurring service or software-like revenue. But an adoption announcement is not the same thing as proof that customers will pay enough, often enough, for Ginkgo to cover the cost of the platform and create value per share.

The latest financial evidence keeps that distinction sharp. Continuing revenue in the second quarter was only a little above twenty million dollars and fell by almost half from the prior year. The adjusted operating loss remained deep, and management still expected full-year cash burn of roughly one hundred twenty-five million to one hundred fifty million dollars. Cash and marketable securities provided several hundred million dollars of liquidity, but some cash was restricted, operating cash use remained heavy, and the company had already issued more shares through an at-the-market program. That means the platform has time to prove itself, but time is being purchased partly with the resources of existing shareholders. The central question is no longer whether automated biology is interesting. It is whether adoption can reverse revenue contraction and lower losses before cash consumption and dilution absorb the upside.

Ginkgo's share price did become less demanding. The market mark fell to about seven dollars and seventy-one cents. Under the accepted scenarios, an adverse outcome is worth roughly three dollars and thirty-three cents, a central outcome about seven dollars and fifty-seven cents, and a favorable outcome about sixteen dollars and forty-seven cents. Weighting those outcomes gives a value a little above eight dollars, so the expected return turned modestly positive. That sounds encouraging until the shape of the payoff is examined. The central estimate is still below the market price, the adverse case loses well over half the investment, and the conservative buy level remains near six dollars and six cents. A narrow moving-average crossover and somewhat unusual volume did not arrive with new evidence of revenue, cash, or customer economics. The lower price improved the arithmetic, but it did not establish a sufficient margin of safety.

Twist Bioscience offers a different kind of biological infrastructure. Instead of operating a broad cell-programming platform, Twist manufactures synthetic DNA and related products used in research, diagnostics, biopharma, and other applications. Its silicon-based manufacturing approach is intended to produce many DNA sequences efficiently, giving customers the physical inputs needed to test biological ideas. Here, the operating evidence is much stronger. Fiscal third-quarter revenue rose by nearly one quarter from the prior year, gross margin moved above one half of revenue, customer shipments grew, and management continued to target adjusted operating breakeven in the following quarter. Twist is still loss-making under standard accounting, but the path from product volume to improving margin is visible.

Two new developments made the story more exciting. First, Twist raised about three hundred twenty-seven million dollars after expenses by issuing new shares. The financing materially extends the runway and lowers near-term funding risk, although it also enlarges the share count. Second, Anthropic selected Twist as one independent evaluator for a campaign involving proteins designed with artificial intelligence. Twist would manufacture proteins and test binding across fifteen targets. This is exactly the kind of bridge the biological automation story needs: a digital design must become a physical molecule, and somebody must make and test it. Yet the disclosure did not reveal contract value, recurring commitments, margins, or downstream drug economics. It validates a use case, not a durable profit pool.

The market treated that use case as highly valuable. Twist rose more than one half over roughly a month, including a gain of more than one fifth in the final session, with heavy volume and an overbought momentum reading. The accepted valuation was updated for the financing and larger share count. Its adverse, central, and favorable estimates are approximately forty-one dollars, seventy dollars, and one hundred four dollars. The market price was about one hundred forty-two dollars. Even the favorable estimate remained well below the quote, while the weighted value was less than half the market price. This is a crucial distinction between a better company and a better stock. Twist can improve its liquidity, grow revenue, expand margin, and participate in an important protein-design experiment while its shares become less attractive because expectations rise much faster than supportable cash economics.

The biology chapter therefore ends with two different forms of patience. Ginkgo Bioworks is currently Hold and Watch. It earns that rating because its autonomous-laboratory option has value and liquidity provides time, but contracting revenue, heavy burn, dilution, severe adverse-case downside, and a central value below the market block action. It could become a Buy if recurring laboratory or data revenue becomes material, cash burn falls credibly, dilution slows, and the price reaches roughly six dollars and six cents or new evidence raises the central value enough to clear the same safety margin. It could become a Sell if revenue continues to contract, customer adoption stalls, burn exceeds guidance, liquidity erodes faster than modeled, or dilution consumes the platform's progress. Twist Bioscience is currently Strong Sell and Avoid. Its growth, margin expansion, financing, and Anthropic work improve the business case, but all accepted valuation outcomes remain below the market. It could become a Buy only if repeatable protein-design and core product economics lift supportable values dramatically, cash profitability becomes durable, dilution moderates, and the price offers a real discount to a rebuilt central case. It remains a Sell, and the negative conclusion strengthens, if adjusted breakeven slips, growth or gross margin disappoints, the Anthropic evaluation fails to become recurring revenue, cash use reaccelerates, or the premium valuation compresses toward the supported range.

The second story moves from biological laboratories to the physical machinery behind artificial intelligence. Software may feel weightless, but large-scale computing consumes turbines, transformers, switchgear, cooling, semiconductor tools, and enormous amounts of capital. Demand must travel through several bottlenecks before a model can run. GE Vernova supplies generation equipment, long-lived services, wind equipment, and grid systems. Applied Materials supplies the deposition, etch, inspection, packaging, and other equipment that helps chipmakers turn advanced designs into working semiconductors. Both companies reported evidence that demand is real. The investment problem is that real demand can already be reflected in the share price, and excellent results can raise the bar faster than they raise value.

GE Vernova's operating picture is formidable. Its second-quarter orders reached more than twenty billion dollars, backlog reached roughly one hundred seventy-six billion dollars, and data-centre orders exceeded five billion dollars for the year to date. Gas turbine capacity and grid equipment are scarce precisely when utilities and developers need more generation and interconnection. The company also produced strong free cash flow and held far more cash than long-term borrowings. This is direct evidence that the artificial-intelligence power bottleneck is moving from forecasts into orders, services, margins, and cash. The installed fleet creates an additional advantage because delivered equipment can generate service revenue for years.

But backlog is not cash in advance without conditions. Customers can delay projects, working capital can reverse, equipment margins can normalize, and the wind business still carries losses and warranty risk. Capacity expansion and acquisitions can also consume returns if the cycle cools. A bullish momentum crossover in the shares followed a decline, lacked unusual-volume confirmation, and faded before the latest review. No newer filing changed the business outlook. The accepted valuation gives an adverse value near five hundred seventy-two dollars, a central value near one thousand sixteen dollars, and a favorable value near one thousand three hundred eighty-six dollars. Against a market price close to nine hundred eighty-seven dollars, the weighted value was only slightly higher than the quote. The company may execute well and still offer little compensation for a disappointing case.

Applied Materials demonstrates the other side of expectations. Its fiscal third quarter was excellent. Revenue exceeded nine billion dollars, operating margin approached one third of sales, earnings per share rose strongly, and operating cash flow exceeded three billion dollars. Semiconductor Systems benefited from leading-edge logic and memory investment, while the services business supplied recurring revenue from the installed base. Management guided the next quarter to revenue above ten billion dollars and strong adjusted earnings. These results support the thesis that more complex chips and advanced packaging require more materials-engineering steps, giving Applied Materials more opportunities to sell tools and services into each generation.

The market response was not celebratory. The shares fell by a little more than five percent in the session after the results on elevated volume and drifted lower over the following days. That does not prove that investors discovered a hidden operating problem. It may instead show that expectations were even higher, that investors questioned the durability of the cycle, or that broader semiconductor positioning changed. The company still faces two large customer concentrations, meaningful exposure to China, export-control uncertainty, equipment cyclicality, and the risk that free cash flow trails accounting earnings over a full cycle. A strong quarter can reduce operating uncertainty while increasing the need to normalize what happens after peak demand.

Most importantly, the new quarter made Applied Materials' old valuation assumptions obsolete. The prior scenario set had used lower normalized earnings evidence. Simply annualizing the latest quarter would be too optimistic, while ignoring it would be stale. The bounded review therefore withdrew the old valuation rather than inventing replacement numbers. That is not indecision; it is a recognition that a price target is only as useful as the assumptions underneath it. A full review must rebuild normalized earnings, diluted shares, balance-sheet adjustments, cycle probabilities, and valuation multiples. Until that work is accepted, there is no defensible buy zone and no honest comparison between the current price and ordered adverse, central, and favorable outcomes.

The infrastructure chapter also ends with two distinct ratings. GE Vernova is currently Hold and Watch. The reason is not weak demand; it is a market price near the weighted value, a central upside of only a few percent, substantial adverse-case downside, and inadequate reward for normalization and execution risk. It could become a Buy if the price falls toward the accepted buy level near eight hundred thirteen dollars while backlog conversion, service attachment, margins, and cash remain strong, or if new evidence raises normalized cash flow without expanding risk. It could become a Sell if cancellations rise, equipment margins deteriorate, wind losses overwhelm power and grid gains, working capital reverses sharply, or the price moves far beyond even favorable assumptions. Applied Materials is currently Unrated and Watch. Record results strengthened the company thesis, but the absence of a current scenario-complete valuation blocks a Buy or Sell call. It could become a Buy only after a full review establishes ordered values, a conservative buy zone, and sufficient downside protection using normalized cycle earnings. It could become a Sell only after that same work shows the market price materially above supportable outcomes, or sooner if export restrictions, customer spending, service growth, margins, or cash conversion deteriorate enough to break the operating thesis. Until then, any precise boundary would be invented.

The final chapter concerns Coinbase and the difference between a better chart and a better business. Coinbase is no longer only a venue for retail speculation. It combines trading, custody, subscriptions, institutional services, stablecoin economics, and blockchain infrastructure. The attractive thesis is that clearer regulation and broader digital settlement create recurring revenue beyond transaction fees. Stablecoins can support payments and round-the-clock settlement, custody can generate fees, and a large compliant platform can distribute products across consumers and institutions. Yet the company remains tied to crypto activity, token prices, interest rates, partner terms, security, regulation, and investor appetite.

The latest fundamental evidence did not change. Second-quarter net revenue was a little above one billion dollars. Transaction revenue and subscription-and-services revenue were almost evenly balanced, while stablecoin revenue was close to three hundred million dollars. That diversification is real, but both major revenue categories declined from the prior year and the company recorded a substantial net loss. Operating cash flow stayed positive over the first half, and the balance sheet retained significant liquidity, yet stock compensation remained large. One counterparty accounted for more than one quarter of quarterly revenue, highlighting how a diversified product list can still contain concentrated economics. Stablecoin growth is valuable only if balance growth, rates, and revenue-sharing terms leave durable cash for shareholders.

Coinbase's chart became more constructive when a momentum measure crossed upward after a modest decline. That signal may reflect improving sentiment, but it did not coincide with a new filing or operating catalyst. The accepted adverse, central, and favorable values remain about twenty-eight dollars, eighty-seven dollars, and one hundred ninety-one dollars. Weighting them gives roughly ninety dollars, compared with a market price near one hundred sixty dollars. The central case implies a large loss, the adverse case implies a devastating loss, and the favorable case offers only modest upside. A bullish crossover cannot change those outcomes unless it is accompanied by evidence that normalized volumes, stablecoin economics, margins, or dilution deserve better assumptions.

Coinbase is therefore currently Strong Sell and Avoid. The platform has meaningful assets and a credible role in digital-finance rails, but cycle sensitivity, counterparty concentration, stock compensation, regulation, and a market price far above weighted and central values make the risk and reward unfavorable. It could become a Buy if recurring subscription, custody, and stablecoin cash earnings remain strong through a crypto downturn, counterparty dependence falls, dilution moderates, and either supportable values rise or the price falls enough to create a substantial margin of safety. It remains a Sell if trading activity contracts, stablecoin balances or partner economics weaken, fees compress, regulation restricts products, a custody or cyber loss occurs, or compensation and dilution continue to absorb per-share cash generation. The chart is worth monitoring, but the business evidence and price still govern the conclusion.

Across all three stories, technological adoption, business quality, valuation, and timing must be kept separate. Ginkgo and Twist show that faster biological workflows do not automatically create recurring economics. GE Vernova shows that a confirmed bottleneck can produce extraordinary orders while leaving little expected return at the current price. Applied Materials shows that excellent results can invalidate old assumptions without immediately supplying new ones. Coinbase shows that improving momentum does not normalize a cyclical platform. None of these conclusions requires predicting that the companies will fail. They require asking whether the current owner is paid enough for the adverse outcome and whether the central case clears a meaningful hurdle.

The broader portfolio remains in cash because each opportunity fails a different gate. That is not a claim that innovation has stopped or that every share must fall. It is an insistence that a compelling narrative cannot substitute for cash conversion, that a strong quarter cannot substitute for normalized valuation, and that a market signal cannot substitute for new fundamental evidence. The most useful question is not which technology will matter. Many of these technologies already matter. The useful question is which company can retain the economics, under what assumptions, and at what price the range of outcomes becomes favorable to the investor.

That's all for today, thank you for listening.
