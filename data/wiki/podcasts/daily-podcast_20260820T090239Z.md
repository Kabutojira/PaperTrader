# Daily research podcast — August 20, 2026

<!-- papertrader-spoken-transcript:start -->

Welcome back to PaperTrader podcast, where the best large language models research investments.

Today we'll talk about... why a sudden surge in an artificial intelligence drug developer can reveal less than it seems, why building semiconductor factories at home does not automatically make a chip stock cheap, and why financing a giant solar project can be good news for the project without resolving the economics of the company behind it. Across all three stories, the central question is the same: when a chart flashes, what evidence turns motion into value?

Begin with Recursion Pharmaceuticals, a clinical-stage biotechnology company trying to change how medicines are discovered. Traditional drug development starts with biological hypotheses, laboratory experiments, and years of testing. Recursion's ambition is to combine large biological data sets, machine learning, and automated laboratories into a faster feedback loop. Software proposes promising relationships between diseases and potential treatments. Robots run experiments at scale. The resulting images and measurements train the next round of models. If that loop selects better candidates earlier, the company could waste less time on weak ideas and create more valuable medicines. But the economic bottleneck is not generating a prediction. It is proving that a treatment is safe, effective, approvable, and commercially useful.

The share price recently offered a dramatic invitation to tell a story. Recursion closed around three dollars and fifty cents after roughly thirty million shares changed hands. Volume was unusually high, the stock gained about thirteen percent in one session, and it rose more than seventeen percent across the measured period. It also moved above its upper statistical trading band. Those facts establish that attention and demand for the shares changed. They do not establish why. A filing search found no newer annual, quarterly, or material event report that identified an operating catalyst. The latest quarterly filing and results exhibit were unchanged, and the two principal studies already known to be recruiting remained in the same status.

This is where market signals must be separated from clinical evidence. A volume anomaly says trading was unusually active compared with the stock's recent history. An upper-band move says the price traveled unusually far above a rolling average. Neither signal can tell us whether a trial enrolled faster, a patient responded, a partner paid a milestone, or a regulator accepted a new path. Sometimes price moves before public evidence because investors anticipate news. Sometimes short covering, options positioning, sector enthusiasm, or ordinary speculation drives the move. Without a disclosed cause, the chart is a prompt to check the business, not permission to invent a breakthrough.

There is genuine progress beneath Recursion's story, but it remains early. Two clinical studies are recruiting. Another candidate has clearance to begin an early-stage trial, and a large pharmaceutical partner advanced an initial neuroscience target into joint discovery. Further data for one program are expected in early November, with another program update expected during the second half. These milestones make the platform testable. They do not yet establish comparative efficacy, approval probability, pricing, market penetration, or the share of future economics that Recursion would retain after partners and financing providers are paid.

The financial runway gives the company time to reach some of those tests, but it is not a free asset waiting for shareholders. At the end of June, cash including restricted cash was about five hundred fifty-seven million dollars. During the first half, operating activities used about one hundred eighty-seven million dollars. Quarterly revenue was less than eight million dollars, while the quarterly net loss was roughly one hundred thirty-one million. Management lowered its cash operating expense guidance and continued to describe runway into early the year after next without new financing. Yet the filing also says substantial losses are expected and additional capital will probably be required. The share count was already rising, and some data-related commitments can be paid with stock. Each new share spreads any future success across more owners.

That combination explains why a conventional valuation would be false precision. A useful drug valuation needs estimates for technical success, regulatory success, remaining development cost, launch timing, commercial pricing, eligible patients, market share, partner milestones, royalties, future financing, and the fully diluted share count when value might finally arrive. The current primary evidence does not bound those inputs tightly enough to order adverse, central, and favorable per-share outcomes. Cash alone cannot stand in for value because the business is expected to spend much of it pursuing the pipeline. A low share price is not synonymous with a low valuation when the future claim count and the probability of success are both uncertain.

Recursion's current accepted rating is Unrated and Watch. It has that rating because the platform is producing candidates and identifiable clinical tests, but clinical translation, recurring economics, financing, dilution, and valuation remain too uncertain for an investable entry. There is no defensible numerical Buy boundary today. It could become a Buy only after primary evidence supports program probabilities and development economics, clinical results demonstrate meaningful benefit with acceptable safety, partnership income or owned-pipeline value becomes material, financing risk is bounded, and a fully diluted valuation offers a real margin of safety. It could become a Sell or Avoid if key trials disappoint, programs are abandoned, partnership revenue stays immaterial, cash burn accelerates, or repeated dilution consumes the value of any scientific progress.

Now turn to Texas Instruments, a much older company operating at almost the opposite end of the corporate life cycle. Texas Instruments makes analog and embedded semiconductors. Analog chips translate real-world signals such as temperature, pressure, sound, voltage, and motion into information electronic systems can use. Embedded processors perform dedicated control tasks inside cars, factories, power equipment, and consumer devices. These products are often inexpensive relative to the system they control, but they can remain in production for many years. That breadth, longevity, and customer diversity can make a strong franchise even when the semiconductor cycle is weak.

The recent chart signal was bearish rather than exuberant. The stock fell a little more than nine percent across the measured period and closed near two hundred sixty-seven dollars, just below its lower statistical band. Momentum also weakened. A lower-band move can attract contrarian investors because it suggests the selloff has become unusually extended compared with recent prices. But statistical distance is not economic cheapness. The latest filings contained no new operating disclosure that explained the move, so the review kept the same business assumptions and asked whether the lower price had created enough prospective return.

Current operations show a business recovering while funding an unusually large investment cycle. Quarterly revenue was around four and a half billion dollars, and net income was close to one and a third billion. Trailing free cash flow was roughly three billion dollars. At the same time, capital spending over the trailing period was about four and a half billion, debt was around fourteen billion, and cash plus short-term investments were below six billion. Management continues to build large wafer fabrication capacity in Texas and Utah, supported in part by federal manufacturing incentives. These factories are designed around large wafers that can lower unit costs when they run at sufficient utilization.

The strategic logic is easy to appreciate. Customers worried about geopolitical concentration may value dependable semiconductor supply from the United States. Government support can reduce the cost of building capacity. Long-lived analog products may fit factories that can produce many different parts for years. That creates a plausible link to nearshoring and friendshoring, the effort to place critical production in domestic or politically aligned regions. Yet a plausible mechanism is not an accepted investment relationship. The missing proof is whether customers award enough durable volume, whether the new plants reach useful utilization, and whether the incentives and cost advantages create returns above the cost of capital rather than merely creating more capacity.

This distinction matters because semiconductor factories impose costs before demand arrives. Buildings, tools, engineers, and qualification work consume cash during construction and ramp. If the industrial and automotive cycle remains soft, new capacity can sit below efficient utilization. Depreciation and interest still appear even when machines are not full. If customers do value domestic supply, Texas Instruments may gain share and lower costs over time. If competitors also expand or end demand disappoints, shareholders may fund years of spending before seeing an adequate return. Strategic importance can support resilience without guaranteeing attractive per-share economics at any quoted price.

The accepted scenarios make the valuation tension concrete. The adverse outcome is about one hundred fifty dollars per share, the central outcome roughly two hundred fifty-seven dollars, and the favorable outcome around three hundred seventy-four dollars. Weighting those outcomes gives a value near two hundred fifty-nine dollars, below the latest market price. The central outcome also sits below the quote. The lower-band decline therefore improved the arithmetic but did not reverse it. A favorable recovery could still produce meaningful gains, while the adverse case would lose more than two fifths. That imbalance is why a famous franchise and a falling stock can still fail the entry test.

A conservative Buy boundary is about two hundred five dollars per share. Reaching that price would provide the required margin of safety against the existing scenario values, but price alone should not do all the work. Evidence that industrial and automotive demand is recovering, that new factories are winning durable customer commitments, that utilization is improving, and that capital spending begins to convert into free cash flow would make the nearshoring mechanism more credible. Conversely, a lower quote accompanied by weaker margins, further inventory correction, or rising debt could simply indicate that the adverse case is becoming more likely.

Texas Instruments is currently Hold and Watch. The rating reflects a durable analog franchise, broad product exposure, a solid balance sheet relative to the investment program, and strategically valuable domestic manufacturing, offset by heavy capital spending, cyclical demand, negative expected return at the current mark, poor adverse-to-central payoff, and an unverified nearshoring relationship. It could become a Buy near or below roughly two hundred five dollars if recovery, factory utilization, customer commitments, and free-cash-flow conversion support the retained values. It could become a Sell or Avoid if the price exceeds even supportable favorable outcomes without stronger economics, or if demand weakness, underused factories, margin compression, rising leverage, subsidy dependence, or capital-allocation mistakes undermine the central case.

The third case is Canadian Solar, whose name conceals three related but economically different activities. The company sells solar modules, develops and owns power projects, and builds battery storage systems. Module manufacturing is highly competitive and exposed to global oversupply, tariffs, trade rules, and rapid price changes. Project development can create value by securing land, interconnection, permits, contracts, and financing, then selling or operating the finished asset. Storage can benefit from growing electricity demand and congested grids because batteries shift power from abundant hours to scarce ones. Growth in one segment does not automatically repair weakness in another.

The latest chart looked encouraging. The shares closed around fifteen dollars and eighty cents after a little more than a two percent gain across the measured period, and short-term momentum crossed above its signal line. This type of crossover can indicate that recent gains are beginning to outrun earlier losses. It is useful as a timing alert, but it does not identify the source of the improvement. A current filing search found no newer quarterly operating report. The most relevant new fundamental item was financing for a very large storage project in Arizona called Cobalt Solar.

Cobalt Solar combines a solar installation with a battery system capable of storing several times its generating capacity for later delivery. Canadian Solar announced about one billion four hundred million dollars of construction financing and a conditional commitment of up to five hundred million dollars of developer equity. It also issued about one hundred million dollars of convertible notes to support project equity needs. This matters because arranging capital can move a project from a plan toward construction, and a completed asset can generate sale proceeds or contracted cash flow. It also shows that lenders and partners were willing to fund a specific project structure.

But project financing is not the same as free corporate cash. Construction debt is tied to project milestones and repayment claims. Developer equity arrives under conditions and gives the provider an economic interest. Convertible notes may eventually become shares or remain debt, depending on their terms and future price. A financing package can reduce near-term execution risk while adding claims ahead of, or alongside, common shareholders. The investor still needs to know the company's retained ownership, required equity contribution, construction risk, tax-credit eligibility, power contract, completion guarantees, and expected return after every financing layer.

The latest quarterly evidence explains why that caution is necessary. Revenue was about one billion two hundred million dollars and the company reported a loss attributable to shareholders. Module shipments fell sharply from the prior year, while battery storage shipments more than doubled. Operating cash use was around two hundred nine million dollars as inventory rose above two billion dollars. Net debt was close to five billion. Reported gross margin benefited from a large tariff refund, so the headline percentage overstated the underlying operating margin. Storage growth and project progress are real positives, but module oversupply, working capital, leverage, financing costs, and one-time benefits still dominate the risk discussion.

The valuation range looks more inviting than Texas Instruments but remains asymmetric. The adverse net-asset outcome is roughly ten dollars and forty cents per share, the central outcome about eighteen dollars and eighty cents, and the favorable outcome a little above twenty-seven dollars. The weighted expected value implies a gain in the low teens from the current price. A conservative entry boundary is about fifteen dollars. The market was only modestly above it. Yet the adverse loss is still large relative to central upside, and the expected gain is not generous enough compared with that downside. The missing accepted relationship to the solar, storage, and grid-flexibility thesis adds another gate: the business must show that sector demand reaches common shareholders through margins and cash, not merely through shipments and financed assets.

That relationship is plausible. Electricity demand is growing, connection queues are congested, and batteries can help balance variable generation. Cobalt Solar offers a concrete test of whether project financing becomes construction, operation, and value realization. Confirmation would include the project reaching milestones without major overruns, storage backlog converting at stable margins, module inventories normalizing, operating cash flow improving, and leverage declining. Contrary evidence would include delayed projects, weak contract economics, tariff or sourcing problems, persistent module losses, larger working-capital drains, or financing that repeatedly transfers upside away from existing shareholders.

Canadian Solar's current accepted rating is Buy and Initiate, but only at watch-tier conviction, and it remains allocation-ineligible. That apparent tension is important: the scenario arithmetic supports a positive classification, yet the payoff ratios and accepted causal-relationship gate still fail, so it is not an actionable purchase. It could become an actual Buy near or below roughly fifteen dollars if the relationship is validated, Cobalt Solar progresses on sound economics, storage growth converts into cash, normalised margins improve, inventory and net debt decline, and the adverse-to-central payoff clears the required threshold. It could become a Sell or Avoid if project financing proves dilutive or uneconomic, module oversupply keeps destroying margin, cash use and leverage worsen, policy or tariff changes impair assets, execution failures damage the project portfolio, or the price rises beyond the favorable value without stronger evidence.

Put the three stories together and the charts almost form a trap. Recursion rose sharply on heavy volume, but no disclosed event changed the clinical or financial evidence. Texas Instruments fell below a statistical band, but the lower quote still exceeded the weighted business value. Canadian Solar produced a bullish momentum crossover and verified project financing, but project-level capital did not erase module losses, working-capital strain, leverage, or downside asymmetry. The technical signals were useful because they told us where to look. None could answer the investment question by itself.

The deeper contrast is how capital must cross a long bridge before it becomes per-share value. At Recursion, cash funds experiments that must survive clinical and regulatory testing before medicines can earn revenue. At Texas Instruments, capital builds factories that need customer commitments and high utilization before scale lowers costs and restores free cash flow. At Canadian Solar, borrowed and partner capital builds energy assets whose contracts, construction, ownership, and financing layers determine what common shareholders retain. In every case, a strategic narrative can be true while the current security remains unattractive or unpriceable.

The practical investor habit is to ask what changed in the cash flows, claims, and probabilities. Did new evidence raise the chance of success? Did financing create value after debt, dilution, and partner rights? Did a lower price create enough compensation for the adverse case? Is the company's connection to a broad theme verified by revenue and cash rather than association? Recursion still needs clinical translation and a supportable valuation. Texas Instruments needs a better price or stronger proof that domestic capacity will earn its cost. Canadian Solar needs cash conversion, healthier downside, and a validated path from grid demand to shareholder value. Patience in all three cases is not indifference. It is the conclusion produced by keeping price motion, business evidence, and valuation separate.

That's all for today, thank you for listening.
